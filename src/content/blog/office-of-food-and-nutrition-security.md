Philadelphia has a food policy advisory council. It does not have a city office whose actual job is food. That sounds like a technicality until you ask a simple question: when SNAP gets cut, when a pantry runs dry in the third week of the month, when a corner of the city loses its only grocery store, whose desk does that land on? Right now the honest answer is a lot of desks, and none of them belong to someone whose whole job is feeding people. On October 8, 2026, a bill was introduced in City Council to change that. I want to walk through where it came from, what it would actually do, and what I am watching for between now and the day voters may get the final say.

## Seventeen months of work, and the first ask was structure

The story starts with City Council's Food and Nutrition Security Task Force. According to the [task force's own report](https://phlcouncil.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/City-of-Philadelphia-FNSTF-Report-Recommendations-06.24.pdf), it was created through Council Resolution #240125-A and held its first meeting on February 5, 2025. Five working groups covered food access, nutrition, food production, emergency food and benefits, and funding and procurement. It was chaired jointly by George Matysik, executive director of [SHARE Food Program](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/news-and-media/food-nutrition-security-task-force-sets-priorities-to-address-food-access/), and Mark Edwards, president and CEO of [The Food Trust](https://thefoodtrust.org/philadelphia-takes-a-major-step-toward-a-more-connected-food-system/). These are people who run food distribution and food access in this city every day, not a consulting team flown in for a deck.

On June 24, 2026, Council published [the task force recommendations](https://phlcouncil.com/philadelphia-city-councils-food-and-nutrition-security-task-force-recommends-new-approach-to-food-access-in-philadelphia/) after 17 months of working groups, public testimony and listening sessions. They come in four areas, and the order matters. Governance and accountability comes first. Then immediate stabilization and resident access. Then food production and economic systems. Then health, education, and human development.

Recommendation one is a permanent, city led coordination office "with clear authority, dedicated staffing, and multi-year funding." The report describes it in one line I keep repeating to people: "This office is the air traffic controller the system currently lacks." It also asks that the office be set up through "a formal charter change that survives political transitions," so it cannot be quietly cut the next time a budget gets tight.

## The problem was never a lack of ideas

I have read a lot of city reports on hunger. Most of them list programs. This one starts with a diagnosis I think is exactly right. In the report's words: "The challenge has never been a lack of ideas. It has been a system with no mechanism to align them."

Philadelphia does have assets. The same report counts community fridges providing more than 200,000 meals a year, more than $2.5 million invested through the Food Justice Initiative, and a ten year urban agriculture plan. The city also has the Food Policy Advisory Council, which the [Office of Sustainability](https://phila.gov/departments/office-of-sustainability/about/commissions-and-councils) houses and which has done real work for years. But an advisory council advises. The report quotes one task force member describing it this way: "It had no teeth." That is not a knock on the people who served on it. It is a description of what an advisory body is built to do.

Meanwhile, the pressure on the system keeps rising. The task force reports that Philadelphia lost more than 25,000 residents from SNAP rolls because of new federal work requirements, and that SNAP Ed nutrition education was eliminated from schools and community sites. About 312,000 Philadelphians, roughly one in five, already face food insecurity according to [Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap](https://www.feedingamerica.org/research/map-the-meal-gap/by-county). When a shock like that hits and nobody owns the whole picture, every pantry, school and clinic responds on its own. People do heroic work that way. It just does not add up to a plan.

## What the bill introduced on October 8 actually does

Council President Kenyatta Johnson said in June that he would introduce legislation in the fall. He did. As [PhillyVoice reported on October 8](https://www.phillyvoice.com/grocery-affordability-philadelphia-city-council/), the package is called the Philly Food Affordability Act, and its centerpiece creates an Office of Food and Nutrition Security. The [Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/politics/philadelphia/council-president-kenyatta-johnson-city-run-grocery-stores-food-insecurity-20261008.html) reported that the charter amendment was introduced by Majority Leader Katherine Gilmore Richardson on Johnson's behalf.

Here is what the [Council announcement](https://phlcouncil.com/philadelphia-city-council-president-kenyatta-johnson-introduces-philly-food-affordability-act-to-help-families-put-food-on-the-table/) lays out. The office would be led by a Director appointed by the Mayor and approved by City Council, with at least five years of senior leadership experience. Its job would include writing citywide food and nutrition policy, coordinating food access across city departments and with community and private partners, collecting and reporting data on food insecurity, preparing for disruptions in the food system and going after additional funding. A Food and Nutrition Security Advisory Commission would advise the Mayor and the office and deliver recommendations by December 31, 2029.

Because this amends the Home Rule Charter, it has two gates. Council has to pass it first. Then voters decide, and the announcement names the May 18, 2027 ballot. If voters say yes, the amendment takes effect on January 1, 2028. The same day the bill was introduced, Council also approved resolutions for hearings on a city supported grocery store and on how grocery chains use customer data and dynamic pricing, as [KYW Newsradio reported](https://www.audacy.com/kywnewsradio/news/local/philly-city-council-food-insecurity).

As of mid November, there has been no Council vote on the charter amendment. [34th Street Magazine](https://www.34st.com/article/2026/11/city-supported-grocery-stores-philadelphia) still described it on November 13 as a proposal that Council has to approve before it goes to voters. I could not find a hearing date or a dollar figure for the office in any public source, so I am not going to put one in this post.

## New York already ran this experiment

The best argument for doing this through the charter comes from two hours up the road. According to a [2022 policy brief from the NY Food 2025 project](https://sph.cuny.edu/brief6-20220315), Mayor Bloomberg created New York City's first Food Policy Coordinator inside the mayor's office in 2008, by Executive Order No. 122. That worked, but an executive order can be undone by the next executive. So in February 2020 the New York City Council passed Local Law 41, which put the Mayor's Office of Food Policy into the city charter. The brief puts the reason plainly: the office is now "protected from being eliminated at the whim of a subsequent executive order."

New York took twelve years to get from a coordinator to a protected office. Philadelphia's task force looked at that kind of history and asked to start at the end point. That is the part of this bill I find most encouraging. It is not another pilot. It is an attempt to build something that outlasts whoever is mayor.

## Why an office matters to someone who just needs groceries

I understand the reaction. If you are standing in a pantry line, an office in City Hall does not put food in your bag this week. The Inquirer said it directly: none of these proposals involve immediate action on food insecurity.

But think about what coordination actually means on the ground. One place that knows which neighborhoods lost the most SNAP households last quarter. One place that can tell a school, a clinic and a food bank they are serving the same families and should plan together. One place that sees the next federal cut coming and has a plan ready before it lands, instead of everyone scrambling separately after. That is the difference between a city that reacts to hunger and a city that manages it.

This is the work the Feed Philly Coalition exists for. We are a [coalition of people doing food access across Philadelphia](https://feedphillycoalition.org), and the gaps we see most often are not gaps in effort. They are gaps in connection.

## What I am watching between now and May

Three things. First, whether Council moves the charter amendment in time for the May 18, 2027 ballot, since the measure needs Council approval before it can go to voters. Second, the money. The task force asked for dedicated operating funds, not one time support, and an office without a budget line is the same coordination problem with a new name. Third, authority. The report asks that the new office be clearly different from the advisory structures that came before it. The Director's real powers will decide whether this becomes the air traffic controller the report describes or another seat at another table.

If you care about this, tell your council member you want the charter amendment passed and funded. And if it reaches the ballot in May, vote.
