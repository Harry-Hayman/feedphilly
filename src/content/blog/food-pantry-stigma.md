I want to start with a question, and I want you to actually answer it before you read on. You have not eaten since yesterday. Your kids had cereal for dinner. There is a food pantry two blocks from your apartment, the food is free, and nobody is going to ask you to prove anything. You walk up, and near the table somebody is holding a phone at chest height, the way people do when they are filming. Maybe it is a volunteer making a post about a good day. Maybe it is a stranger in line. Either way, there is a real chance your face and your bag of groceries end up online by tonight, where your landlord, your coworkers or your kid's teacher could scroll past them. Would you still walk in? I am Harry Hayman, I work on food access with Feed Philly Coalition, and I think a lot of our neighbors would answer no. The research says the fear of being judged is real, and it says something about what we can do about it.

## The Fear Has a Name

The scenario is a hypothetical. I have not found a study that measures how many people skip a pantry because of a phone camera, and I am not going to pretend one exists. But the feeling underneath it is one of the best documented things in the research on food help, and it has a name.

Researchers call it anticipated stigma. [Healthy Eating Research defines it](https://healthyeatingresearch.org/2025/12/how-stigma-impacts-participant-experiences-with-food-security-programs/) as "the fear of being judged for using food security programs." It is the judgment you brace for before anyone has said a word to you. When a team funded by Healthy Eating Research went through 99 published studies on food insecurity stigma in the United States, that fear showed up as a theme in 59.6 percent of them, more often than people describing rude treatment they actually received (35.4 percent) or shame they turned on themselves (20.2 percent). The full review ran in [the American Journal of Health Promotion](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08901171251375977), and [Healthy Eating Research keeps a summary page for it](https://healthyeatingresearch.org/research/food-insecurity-related-stigma-among-adults-in-the-united-states-a-scoping-review/).

I want to be careful with that number, because it gets quoted wrong. It does not mean six in ten people at a pantry are afraid of being judged. It means that in six of every ten studies on this subject, researchers found that fear. That is a statement about how consistently it turns up, across different cities, different teams and different years. I actually find that more striking than a single survey would be. Researchers kept going looking for barriers, and this is the one they kept finding.

## A Mask, a Hoodie and the Neighbors

Here is where my hypothetical stops being hypothetical. The University of Delaware team behind that review runs [a Food Stigma Research Lab](https://www.cresp.udel.edu/labs/food-stigma-food-insecurity-research/), and on its own page it quotes a pantry client from Pennsylvania, a 46 year old woman, in her own words:

> "I don't like my neighbors or people to see me at the pantry. I will put on a face mask, I will pull my hair back and put my hoodie on. If a representative from every family in the town went to the pantry, I still will probably be a little embarrassed."

Read that again. Nobody filmed her. Nobody posted anything. She plans a disguise anyway, because the possibility of being seen is enough. Now put a phone camera in that room and think about what it does to her math.

The same team also sat down with [18 emergency food program clients in Pennsylvania and Delaware](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12671575/) between August and December of 2024 and asked them about the way pantries are run. The study, also published in [the Delaware Journal of Public Health](https://djph.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/djph-114-04.pdf), found long waits, little say over what food people got, physical barriers for people with disabilities, and food that was close to spoiling. The authors wrote that these issues "led to the erosion of autonomy and dignity and perpetuated clients' feelings of shame, frustration, and discomfort." That is not a story about rude volunteers. It is a story about how the room is built.

## Why This Is a Philadelphia Problem Right Now

This would matter in a calm year. This is not a calm year. [Billy Penn reported in May](https://billypenn.com/2026/05/18/snap-cuts-philly-food-banks-strained/) that Share Food Program, which it calls the leading hunger relief organization in the Philadelphia region, had lost around 10 million dollars in federal funding, and that in late October and November of 2025 it saw a nine fold increase in visits, many from people coming for the first time.

Sit with that last part. First timers. People who have never stood in a pantry line before, who do not know what the room looks like, who do not know whether anyone will film them. Those are exactly the people for whom the fear is loudest, because they have nothing to measure it against. The same article notes that part of Share's mission is reducing the stigma around asking for help, which tells me the people running the region's leading hunger relief organization see the same thing I do.

When need climbs this fast, every household that turns around at the door is food that never reaches a kitchen. Nobody counts them, because they never registered. A broken freezer shows up on a report. A frightened neighbor does not.

## What Actually Changes the Answer

So what turns that no into a yes? The good news is that the research is practical here, not preachy. [Healthy Eating Research's summary](https://healthyeatingresearch.org/2025/12/how-stigma-impacts-participant-experiences-with-food-security-programs/) looked at 46 interventions aimed at food insecurity stigma, and 89 percent of them worked on structure rather than on attitudes. Moving food banks to more central locations. Shifting from prepackaged bags to pantry choice or grocery models, where you walk through and pick what your family actually eats. Improving accessibility. Cutting wait times in lines. On the people side, the strategies are things like cultural humility training and codes of conduct, shared agreements among staff and volunteers to serve every person with respect and dignity.

That client choice piece came up in the interviews too. One participant in Delaware said she would "definitely prefer choosing my own" groceries over the prepackaged drive through, because otherwise there is always food she cannot use, and she does not like to waste anything. Choice is not a luxury. It is how a grocery run feels normal.

And then there is the camera. I understand why volunteers take pictures. A full table and a long line feel like proof that the work matters, and donors like to see it. But the people in that line did not sign up to be the proof. Some food banks already treat this as policy. [Vermont Foodbank tells its volunteers](https://www.vtfoodbank.org/faq-items/can-i-take-photos-while-volunteering/) that "photos and videos that capture program participants, their vehicles, or any identifying details need to be pre-approved by Vermont Foodbank staff and permission must be granted by the person(s) being photographed or recorded." That is one sentence, and it costs nothing to adopt.

## What Feed Philly Coalition Is Asking For

Here is what I am asking every Feed Philly Coalition partner site to do. These are our asks, not findings from a study, and I would rather be honest about that than dress them up.

First, ask before any photo. No pictures or video of the people we serve unless they say yes, and nothing that shows a face, a car or a license plate without that yes. If you want a photo for a newsletter, photograph the volunteers, the shelves, the hands sorting produce. The story of the work does not need anyone's face who did not offer it.

Second, track one number alongside the totals you already report: the share of registered households who come back after their first visit. Distribution totals tell you what left the building. A return rate tells you whether people felt safe enough to come back. If that number is low, something in the room is telling people not to return, and you will not find out what by counting bags.

Third, when you can, move toward choice. Not every site can rebuild its layout this year, and I am not pretending otherwise. But a table where people pick from what is there, instead of a bag someone else packed, is a change most sites can make with the food they already have.

I keep coming back to the woman with the hoodie. She still goes. She is braver than the system she is walking into, and she should not have to be. Answer my question one more time, but from behind the table this time. What would it take for her to leave the hoodie at home?
