Stop. Drop. Open up. Shop. That is my rule when I see a pop up produce market, and the other day I followed it. A grey canopy over long folding tables, cardboard boxes of fruit and vegetables lined up end to end, cabbages piled in their own case, a white bucket at the foot of one tent leg, and old brick rowhouses standing behind the whole thing. I am Harry Hayman, and I walked away with papaya, mango, cabbage, and more than I had planned to carry. One of my photos is nothing but a box of mangoes, red and green, the little stickers still on. That is the point of a stand like this one. It is there, it is fresh, and you can be holding a bag of food in the time it takes to find a parking space. What I keep thinking about since is everything that happens before and after that minute.

## Why a stand on the corner matters

I love that vendors take it upon themselves to bring fresh produce into neighborhoods where it can be hard to find. Nobody assigned them that job. They load up, they set up, and they sell.

The need is not small. The City's Board of Health wrote in [an August 2025 post on farmers markets](https://www.phila.gov/2025-08-06-farmers-markets-promote-access-to-fresh-fruits-and-vegetables/) that more than 210,000 Philadelphians faced food insecurity in 2023, which it put at over 13 percent of the population. The same post says 22 percent of Black households and 23 percent of Hispanic households lack regular access to nutritious foods, and that a quarter of children lack secure food access at home.

Those numbers are why I stop. A box of mangoes at the curb is not a policy, but it is food that showed up where it was needed, sold by somebody who took the risk of bringing it. I would like the city to treat that risk as something worth backing.

## What Philadelphia already publishes

To be fair to the city, there is a lot out there already, and some of it is good.

The [City's farmers market finder](https://www.phila.gov/farmers-markets/) lets you put in a ZIP code and see markets near you. [PhillyVoice reported](https://www.phillyvoice.com/farmers-market-finder-tool-philadelphia-produce-fruit-vegetables/) that the Department of Public Health launched it in May 2023, that it shows each market's schedule, hours and payment options, and that every early spring the city sends a survey to market operators to keep it current. The [City's service page for finding a farmers market](https://www.phila.gov/services/mental-physical-health/find-a-farmers-market/) says you can see which locations take SNAP, the Farmers Market Nutrition Program and Food Bucks. Per the Board of Health post, the city has more than 30 farmers markets and farm stands, and 56 percent of them accept SNAP and Philly Food Bucks.

There is also a separate [food and meal finder](https://www.phila.gov/services/mental-physical-health/find-free-food-or-meals/), at phila.gov/food, for picking up groceries, getting a ready to eat meal or applying for benefits.

Then there are the market operators themselves. [The Food Trust lists its farmers markets](https://thefoodtrust.org/what-we-do/farmers-markets/) with three that run all year: Clark Park on Saturdays, Fairmount on Thursdays and Headhouse on Sundays, plus seven partner markets. Its [Food Bucks program](https://thefoodtrust.org/what-we-do/food-bucks/), launched in 2010 with the Philadelphia Department of Public Health, gives a $2 coupon for fresh fruits and vegetables for every $5 spent with SNAP at participating markets. [Farm to City](https://www.farmtocitymarkets.com/about) has run producer only farmers markets here since 2000 and says it operated 12 of them in 2023, six open all year.

So here is my honest read. If you want a farmers market, Philadelphia can tell you where one is. If you want a free meal, the city has a map for that too. But the finder is built from a survey of market operators, and a vendor working under a canopy, with no market manager behind them, has no obvious way into that survey. I would like the stand where I bought my papaya to have one.

## The rules a produce vendor works under

Philadelphia did make one part of this easier. The Department of Public Health's guide, [How to Sell Fresh Fruits and Vegetables Outside Your Store](https://www.phila.gov/media/20181129122553/How-to-Sell-Fresh-Fruits-and-Vegetables.pdf), tells store owners they need no special license to put produce out front. The rules are specific: whole fruits and vegetables only, covered by a canopy or an umbrella, no boxes on the ground, the stand within three feet of the building and never at the curb, and at least six feet of clear sidewalk, or half the sidewalk if that is wider.

I laughed when I read the display tips in that same guide. It suggests stocking items that do well without refrigeration, and the list includes mangoes and papayas. My shopping bag, more or less, came straight off the Health Department's list.

A vendor without a storefront lives in a different part of the code. The city's [vending and street sales page](https://www.phila.gov/services/permits-violations-licenses/get-a-license/business-licenses-permits-and-approvals/vending-and-street-sales/) lists the options: a sidewalk sales license, a vendor motor vehicle license, a pushcart license, a vendor on foot license, a curb market license for the Italian Market and a license for special vending districts. The [Sidewalk Sales License](https://www.phila.gov/services/permits-violations-licenses/get-a-license/business-licenses-permits-and-approvals/vending-and-street-sales/get-a-sidewalk-sales-license/) costs $330 per location and $330 again at each renewal, food sales need approval from the Department of Public Health and a food establishment license for a location that is not permanent, and the city limits or prohibits vending on certain streets.

Read all of that together and you see what the rules do. They tell a vendor where they may not stand and what they must pay. Nothing in them says where a produce vendor should stand, how often, or how a shopper is supposed to find out.

## A city that sent produce where it was missing

New York tried something close to what I am describing. In 2008, the City Council and Mayor Bloomberg signed Local Law 9, which created 1,000 permits for Green Carts, mobile fruit and vegetable businesses. According to [a 2012 article in the Journal of Urban Health](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3531353/) the carts could move freely only inside zones where at least 14 percent of residents reported eating no fruits or vegetables the day before. By May 2012 there were 477 active permits, a little under half the number created. The authors reported that from 2008 to 2009, the share of food establishments selling both fruits and vegetables tended to rise in Green Cart precincts and not in comparison precincts.

Notice what New York did. It decided the produce vendor was worth a permit of its own, and it pointed that permit at the blocks that needed it most. The city drew those zones from what residents reported about their own eating. I would go one step further and let the people on those blocks help choose.

New York also does the calendar part. Its health department publishes [one annual list of farmers markets across all five boroughs](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/dataset/NYC-Farmers-Markets/8vwk-6iz2) on the city's open data portal, with the day each market runs, its hours and whether it takes EBT. The 2026 list has 103 entries. One file, one place, and anybody can build a map or a text alert on top of it.

## Three asks, and why each one pays twice

I would love to see Philly do more to make this work reliable. Here is what I mean, in plain terms.

Give vendors regular locations. Not a permanent building, just a spot that is theirs on a given day, so the stand is on the same corner next week.

Publish one citywide calendar people can actually find. The city already surveys market operators every spring. Add the produce vendors who keep a regular stop, and put them on the same map with the same payment details.

Get neighborhood organizations and residents involved in deciding where the stops should be and when people can get to them. A stop that opens while everybody is at work is not much of a stop. The people who live there know the bus schedule, the shift changes and which corner already has foot traffic.

It is a conversation we keep coming back to at [Feed Philly Coalition](https://feedphillycoalition.org/). When somebody knows they can buy fresh produce on the same corner next week, they can plan dinner around it. The vendor can plan inventory around it, too. That is why I say each ask pays twice. Reliability is worth something to the family making a grocery list and to the person deciding how many cases of cabbage to load. A calendar does not only help shoppers find vendors. It helps vendors find customers who come back.

And yes, stop and buy something when you see them.
