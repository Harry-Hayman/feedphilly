Look at the shopfront in the photograph behind our poster. It is a small greengrocer in Ota ward, Tokyo, photographed in October 2017: a wooden door with frosted glass panes, a crowd of potted plants on the step, a ceramic tanuki statue keeping watch by the entrance, and a green shopping street banner hanging beside the door. On a lattice to the left, a row of hand painted wooden boards lists what happens inside. One of them reads 子ども食堂, kodomo shokudo, a children's cafeteria. That shop is Kimagure Yaoya Dandan, and the cafeteria its owner opened there in 2012 gave a whole movement its name. Japan now counts 12,602 of these tables, and it counts them the way a city counts fire stations or bus stops. On 25 November the national support center publishes its 2026 count. We think Philadelphia should be paying attention, both to the counting and to the warning that came from the woman who started it.

## A table a child can walk into alone

Hiroko Kondo is a dental hygienist who ran a vegetable shop in Ota ward. In [an interview with Toyo Keizai Online in May 2025](https://toyokeizai.net/articles/-/880254?page=2), she explained how the cafeteria began. In 2010 a vice principal from a nearby elementary school came in to shop and told her about a pupil whose mother was unwell, a child who ate one banana a day apart from school lunch. The two of them talked about how good it would be if that child could eat at the shop. Kondo called on people she knew locally and started a cafeteria built on one idea: a place where a child can come in alone without anyone finding it strange.

[Prudential's Mimosa Magazine](https://mimosa-mag.prudential.co.jp/_ct/17751558) dates the start to 2012, in Ota ward, at a time when the word kodomo shokudo did not exist yet, and describes the name as meaning a cafeteria that is easy for a child to enter alone. At first, Kondo told Toyo Keizai, it was simply a place to eat together with children, not a support program.

Today the national definition still sounds like that first table. [Musubie, the national kodomo shokudo support center](https://musubie.org/en), counts any free or low cost cafeteria that a child can come to alone, whatever it calls itself: kodomo shokudo, community cafeteria, or a cafeteria for everyone.

## How Japan got to 12,602

Musubie has run a national count every year since 2018. The [final figures for 2025, published in March 2026](https://musubie.org/news/press/30036), put the total at 12,602, one more than the preliminary count released in December. The [December release](https://musubie.org/news/press/28676) noted that the number had grown by more than 1,700 for the third year in a row.

A few comparisons from [Musubie's summary of the results](https://musubie.org/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Shiryou_1_.pdf) make the scale easier to picture. There are now more kodomo shokudo than Japan's 9,236 public junior high schools, combining junior high schools and the combined elementary and junior high schools, and the total is close to 70 percent of the 18,545 public elementary schools counted the same way. Those school figures come from the [national School Basic Survey run by the education ministry](https://www.e-stat.go.jp/stat-search/files?page=1&toukei=00400001&tstat=000001011528). Musubie also estimates about 25.33 million visits a year, 17.32 million of them by children. That is an estimate built from an average attendance figure in its separate survey of operators, multiplied across every confirmed table, and Musubie says so plainly.

What we like most is the humility in the method. The count is run with regional kodomo shokudo networks, prefectural governments and prefectural social welfare councils, and staff sometimes phone individual towns to check. Because nobody has to register a kodomo shokudo with the authorities, Musubie describes its number as the count it could confirm, at least this many. That is an honest way to publish a civic statistic.

## Counting districts, not dinners

Here is the part that changed how we think about community meals. Musubie does not only count tables. It asks what share of elementary school districts has at least one, and it calls that the coverage rate. In 2025 the national figure was 39.69 percent. Okinawa led at 65.89 percent, Tottori followed at 64.96 percent and Tokyo stood at 60.00 percent, while four prefectures were still below 25 percent.

Musubie's stated goal is at least one kodomo shokudo in every elementary school district, and Japan has 18,545 public elementary schools. The reason is written into its own analysis, and it is the sentence we keep repeating to each other: "It is not easy for an elementary school child to walk alone beyond their own school district." (Our translation.)

That reframes the question. A city can have plenty of meal sites and still have whole neighborhoods where a nine year old cannot reach one on foot. Counting by school district turns a list of addresses into a map of gaps. Musubie even publishes a second measure, tables per 100,000 residents, because school mergers can make the district rate look better than it is. The idea has reached government too: the [2025 survey overview](https://musubie.org/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Soukatsu_2025.pdf) notes that Japan's Digital Agency added the number of kodomo shokudo to its regional wellbeing indicators.

## What Wakayama did

Some prefectures treated the goal as a plan. Wakayama, on the Kii Peninsula south of Osaka, had the fastest growth in the 2025 count, up 81.71 percent in a single year. In a comment published with the [December results](https://musubie.org/news/press/28676), the prefecture's children's future division described what it did. It adopted the goal of a kodomo shokudo in every elementary school district. Since the 2023 fiscal year it has expanded a subsidy that pays for equipment and building repairs for new tables. It set up a working team to push the effort across the prefecture, and since 2024 a prefecture wide support network has made it easier to share food and donations. Then it placed a kodomo shokudo officer in every one of its regional bureaus, so that someone close by could work with towns and local welfare councils and take some of the worry out of opening a new table.

None of that is exotic. A target, a small grant, a coordinator in each region and a network that moves food. Every one of those pieces has an equivalent in Philadelphia.

## The harder numbers

The same reports are frank about strain. About 600 tables on the previous year's list were not counted in 2025. Musubie cautions that this does not always mean a closure: some asked not to be listed, and some stopped serving meals but kept running tutoring. Still, in its 2025 survey of operators, 61.9 percent said rising prices had increased their costs, and some reported that grants were getting harder to win because so many more tables now apply.

That brings us to the date on our calendar. Musubie will present its 2026 count on Wednesday 25 November, with results posted on its website that afternoon, Tokyo time. Its [event notice](https://musubie.org/news/seminar-event/35101) says the 2026 results are due that day. We will read the full figures when they come out.

## The founder's warning

The most useful voice in this story may be the one most skeptical of it. In spring 2025 Kondo announced on Facebook that she was drawing a line between herself and the wider kodomo shokudo movement. She explained why to [Toyo Keizai Online](https://toyokeizai.net/articles/-/880254), in an interview by Haru Sugiyama. These are our translations.

> The image that kodomo shokudo help end child poverty, that they are a good thing, has spread too far.

> Serving a meal once or twice a month, or even once a week, or handing over two or three kilos of rice, changes nothing about child poverty.

> Child poverty will not be solved unless national and local governments take seriously the problems of parents' work, children's education, housing and the like.

Later in the same interview she said she had thought from the very beginning that it would be best if kodomo shokudo were no longer needed.

We take that seriously, because it is our argument too. A table is a relationship, a hot meal and a safe hour. It is not a wage, a lease or a benefit check. A count of tables only helps if it sits next to the harder work.

## What Philadelphia could count

Philadelphia already has the bones of this. The City runs a [Food and meal finder](https://www.phila.gov/food/) that lets anyone search by address or ZIP code for food sites, general meal sites, older adult meal sites, student meal sites and public benefits help. At food sites and general meal sites any resident is eligible, with no ID or proof of income required. Every student in the School District of Philadelphia gets free breakfast and lunch at school.

What we have not seen is Musubie's number: the share of elementary school catchments where a child can walk to a free community table outside school hours. So here is a proposal, and it is ours, not an announcement from any partner. [Feed Philly Coalition](https://feedphillycoalition.org/) could run Philadelphia's first table count. Church halls, recreation centers, mutual aid suppers, after school meal programs, community fridges with a table beside them. Map every one against elementary school catchments. Publish the count every Thanksgiving week, and use the gaps to decide where money, food supply and volunteers go next.

And we would keep Kondo's warning printed on the same page. Wages, housing and SNAP are not questions a table can answer, and a good count should make that louder, not quieter.

If you run a table in this city, or eat at one, we want to hear where it is. That is how a count starts. You can also follow Musubie's work on [Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/musubie_npo/).
