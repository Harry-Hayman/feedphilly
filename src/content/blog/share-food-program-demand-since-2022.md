Let me start with who we are talking about, because a lot of people in this city use Share Food Program's food without ever hearing its name. Share is a food bank based in North Philadelphia, and [Billy Penn](https://billypenn.com/2026/05/18/snap-cuts-philly-food-banks-strained/) describes it as the leading hunger relief organization in the region and one of the biggest independent food banks in the nation. Most of what it moves does not leave through its own front door. It goes out through partners: food pantries, community groups, senior sites and hundreds of schools across the five county area. So when Share says the load has changed, it is telling you something about the whole system that sits on top of it. And Share has been saying exactly that, with one number, for a while now. I think that number deserves a closer look, and so does one answer to it that is already standing in a suburban warehouse.

## What Share Food Program Is

Share started in 1986 as a food co-op, according to [its own history page](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/about-us/our-history-and-mission/), so it turned forty this year. Its executive director, George Matysik, has run it since March 2019. The organization feeds people in a few different ways at once. Back in 2024, [The Philadelphia Citizen](https://thephiladelphiacitizen.org/unacceptable-work-share-food/) reported that Share was getting lunch to more than 300,000 students across 800 schools and 70 school districts, and that its Senior Box program delivered 32 pounds of free food to more than 7,000 older adults every month. In April 2025, [Billy Penn's reporting on federal cuts](https://billypenn.com/2025/04/21/local-food-bank-share-food-program-usda-cuts/) described a network of about 400 organizations and about 800 schools reaching hundreds of thousands of people each week.

That matters for how you read everything below. When need rises at Share, it is not one pantry with a longer line. It is a distribution backbone carrying more weight for hundreds of partners at the same time.

## The Number Matysik Keeps Giving

Here is the figure. In December 2025, Matysik told [The Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/philly-first/changemaker-spotlight/pf-philly-gives-share-food-program-george-matysik-20251203.html) that Share had seen "about 120% increase from 2022 to 2025, because many of those resources have been pulled from folks." He gave Billy Penn the same number in April 2025. And in April 2024, Share had already told The Philadelphia Citizen it was seeing a 98 percent increase in need since 2022. Put those side by side and you get the shape of it: 98 percent by the spring of 2024, about 120 percent by 2025. The line kept going up.

I want to be careful with this. About 120 percent is Matysik's own count of how much heavier the load at his organization has become, and that is how I report it. It is not an audited regional total. But it comes from the person closest to the intake numbers, given more than once, months apart, and it lines up with the city's own picture. A 2025 report from the Office of Homeless Services, cited by Billy Penn in May 2026, found food insecurity in Philadelphia almost reaching 20 percent.

## What Got Cut While Need Climbed

The other half of the story is money. Matysik told [KYW Newsradio](https://www.audacy.com/kywnewsradio/news/local/philadelphia-food-banks-usda-federal-funding-cuts) in March 2025 that "about 60% of our total food and funding comes from federal sources." A few weeks later Billy Penn reported that Share had lost about $8 million in food and funding to federal cuts. By May 2026, according to Matysik, the loss had reached around $10 million, and the organization was straining to fill the gaps as more and more people came to the pantry.

Share is not alone. In the same Billy Penn piece, Matysik said the 18 food banks in Pennsylvania have seen a 40 percent increase in need over the last two years. I am attributing that to him rather than presenting it as an independent statewide tally, because that is where it comes from. The same reporting adds two more pressures: as of February 2026, 27 percent of the state's population was enrolled in SNAP, the federal grocery benefit most of us still call food stamps, and around 45,000 recipients were at risk of losing access after federal SNAP cuts. Stigma sits on top of all of it. A 2025 study by Healthy Eating Research found that nearly 60 percent of people fear being judged for using food assistance.

None of this reads to me as a story about anyone at Share doing something wrong. When a public support gets thinner, the need does not vanish. It moves to whoever still has a door open, and in this region that is very often Share and its partners.

## A Surge That Will Not End Is a Load

This is the part I keep turning over. We talk about hunger in the language of emergencies: a surge, a spike, a crisis appeal before the holidays. Emergencies get one time money, and one time money assumes the thing will end. A rise that starts in 2022 and is still climbing in 2025 is not a spike. It is the new normal weight on the system, and a normal weight needs normal infrastructure: floor space, coolers, freezers, loading docks, trucks and the people who run them.

You cannot sign a long lease, buy a freezer or hire drivers for the long term against a grant that might not come back in twelve months. That is the trap. The need is permanent and the money is temporary. And the [Inquirer piece](https://www.inquirer.com/philly-first/changemaker-spotlight/pf-philly-gives-share-food-program-george-matysik-20251203.html) already notes, in the interviewer's own framing, that Share's staff and cold storage capacity grew five fold after Matysik took over in 2019. Share has been building the backbone. The question is whether the funding treats it like one.

## Montgomery County Already Made the Bet

Here is the example I did not know about until I went looking, and it is the reason I wanted to write this. On January 29, 2026, [Share Food Program and Montgomery County opened a 27,000 square foot warehouse](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/events/press-release-share-food-program-unveils-new-warehouse-in-montgomery-county/) at 1111 N. Broad Street in Lansdale, in a building that used to house an electric supply company. Montgomery County Commissioner Neil K. Makhija put it plainly at the ribbon cutting: "The County invested $7 million in this facility."

Look at what the money bought. A cooler and a freezer. Two loading docks. Room for about 200 pallets of dry goods and about 100 each of frozen and refrigerated food. A hub for emergency food, senior boxes, school lunches and rescued food, serving more than 70 food pantries, senior sites, school districts and community agencies. Matysik's line that day was simple: "This warehouse helps us meet people where they are." It was not Share's first move outside the city either. In June 2025 [KYW reported](https://www.audacy.com/kywnewsradio/news/local/share-food-program-expands-in-delco-with-new-warehouse) that Share had opened a warehouse in Holmes, Delaware County, to supply 15 or 16 pantries there.

That is a county paying for food access the way it pays for a road or a firehouse. You build it once, properly, and you plan on using it for years. It is not glamorous and it does not trend. It is exactly the kind of investment a rise in need like this one calls for.

## What Philadelphia Could Do Next

So what about the city Share calls home? On November 5, 2026, Share published [a policy update](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/policy-advocacy/midterm-updates-on-food-access-policy-at-the-city-state-and-federal-level/) saying it "has its transfer request lodged with the Council President," while "the possibility of a Mid-Year Transfer is still being debated between Council and the Administration." The update lists City Council's last regular session of the year as December 10. It does not say how much Share asked for or what for, so I will not pretend to know. But the timing is real, and the decision sits with people we can call.

My ask, as someone who works alongside these organizations through [Feed Philly Coalition](https://feedphillycoalition.org), is not clever. Fund the backbone like a backbone. Multi year operating money instead of one more emergency grant. Cold storage, docks and trucks treated as public infrastructure, because that is what they already are. Montgomery County showed what that looks like with a building you can drive past. Philadelphia, home to what Billy Penn has called the region's largest hunger relief organization, could make the same bet.

If you want to help right now, Share runs a volunteer program, which Billy Penn's May piece walks through, and you can find it at [sharefoodprogram.org](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/). And if you live in Philadelphia, your Councilmember's office is a phone call away before December 10.
