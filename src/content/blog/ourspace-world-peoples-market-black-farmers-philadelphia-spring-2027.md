On the second Saturday of the month, a farmers market on wheels is scheduled to pull into the Love My Largo Market in Upper Marlboro, Maryland, and stay from ten in the morning until two. The vegetables on board come from Black farmers and a few small farms in the region, and the people running the truck say it is committed to food justice. I am Harry Hayman, and I have spent more time than my team would like to admit thinking about what that truck represents. A few days ago I connected with the people behind OurSpace World and The People's Market, and I liked what I heard. Their Black Food Truck Tour was going to stop in Philadelphia on November 14 and 15. That visit has been postponed to Spring 2027, with no date set. I would rather treat that as time to prepare than as a delay.

## What the Black Food Truck is, in its own words

The [Black Food Truck's own page](https://www.blackfoodblackfutures.com/the-black-food-truck) calls it "a farmers market on wheels committed to food justice." It says the truck supports local agriculture and builds connections across Washington, DC, Maryland and Virginia. It also says the truck collaborates with local Black farmers, and some small farm operators, and that the produce is picked up from the farm and made accessible to the community.

I keep reading that last part. Picked up from the farm. A lot of food programs begin with a warehouse and a pallet. This one begins with a grower and a pickup, and the customer sits at the other end of a short line. The page also says the truck has partnered with The People's Market for a relaunch during the 2026 market season. I can tell you only what the page says about The People's Market: it is the partner in that relaunch. Public coverage is thin, so I am not going to guess beyond it.

The page lists a calendar of Maryland and DC stops from the summer into the fall, so this is a truck that works a route. That matters to me. A market that shows up on a schedule gives a farmer something to plan a harvest around.

## What OurSpace World does

The [OurSpace World website](https://www.ourspaceworld.org/) describes three programs: the Ujima Initiative, which organizes groups committed to living and working collaboratively; Project Teach22, which trains what it calls land stewards and earth healers; and the Calabash, which directs resources to communities. [The organization's own history](https://www.ourspaceworld.org/who-we-are.html) says it was established in 2011 and that in 2018 it started farming and gardening initiatives.

The Calabash is the part that speaks most directly to people who grow food for a living. [Its page](https://www.ourspaceworld.org/calabash.html) says it offers technical assistance with grants and loans, business planning, partnership development and fiscal sponsorship, and that it has supported projects including land purchase, farm infrastructure, food safety and produce aggregation. It names Black and Brown farmers, growers, food producers and consultants as the people it serves. The page says it has directed over 1.5 million dollars to mission aligned groups. That is the organization's own figure, so I am passing it along as theirs.

Look at that list again. Land. Infrastructure. Food safety. Aggregation. None of that is a food drive. It is the unglamorous plumbing that lets a small grower sell to someone who is not standing at the farm gate.

There is a classroom side, too. The [Africultural Connections curriculum](https://www.ourspaceworld.org/africulturalconnections.html) comes out of Teach22 and aims to bridge the gap between the farm and the classroom. OurSpace World built it with a fifth generation farmer, Michael Carter Jr. of Carter Farms, and a curriculum designer, and the page says the first version launched in late 2023.

## A festival that put farmers at the center

In October 2023, Prince George's Community College hosted the [Black Food, Black Futures Festival](https://www.pgcc.edu/about-pgcc/events/event-details/black-food-black-futures-festival.php) on October 6 and 7. The college's event page says the African American Studies Institute hosted it with OurSpace World Inc. and that it was funded by a Prince George's Community Partnership Grant. The goal, as the page puts it, was to celebrate Black food culture and raise awareness of food insecurity in the county, and it promised Black farmers and gardeners as vendors, instructors and herbalists.

[WTOP's coverage that week](https://wtop.com/prince-georges-county/2023/10/prince-georges-community-college-hosting-black-food-festival) added a detail I like. Dr. Iyelli Ichile, the history and African American studies professor quoted in the story, said the festival had experts who were not just people who cook food. It also included panels, workshops, cooking demonstrations and a food drive with the Capital Area Food Bank. Notice the pairing: a celebration and a food drive in one weekend. Nobody pretended that culture and hunger were separate subjects.

## Why a market beats a meal on its own

Here is where my own thinking comes in. In Philadelphia we do a lot of feeding, and we should. People are hungry today, and a hungry person does not need a lecture on economic structure before dinner. But I have said this before and I will say it again: we cannot feed our way out of food insecurity.

A donated meal ends when the meal is eaten. A grower who finds a steady buyer, a chef who finds a reliable supplier and a neighbor who can buy fresh food at a fair price all keep going after the truck leaves. What interests me about the OurSpace World model, as far as the public record shows it, is that the money has a destination. The truck says it works with local Black farmers and picks up from the farm. The Calabash says it helps with business planning and aggregation. Put those two ideas next to each other and the question becomes obvious. Who is on the buying end?

That is the part a single weekend cannot answer, and it is the part Philadelphia can actually work on. We have farmers, and we have restaurants, institutions and businesses that buy food. We have neighborhoods that need better access to fresh, affordable food. What we are often missing is the introduction.

## What Feed Philly Coalition hopes to do with the time

I should be careful here, because I do not want to promise more than we have. [Feed Philly Coalition](https://feedphillycoalition.org/) describes itself as a coalition of nonprofits, businesses, government, healthcare, education, agriculture, philanthropy and community leaders working toward a stronger, healthier and more resilient regional food system. Along with our friends at [SOUTH Restaurant and Jazz Club](https://www.southjazzkitchen.com/), we are looking forward to exploring how we can help make some connections. That is the whole commitment. It is not a sponsorship, it is not a financial pledge and it is not a finished partnership. It is an offer to introduce good people doing good work and see what we can build together.

Spring 2027 is far enough away to do this properly. Imagine the visit as the day relationships get tested rather than the day they begin. A farmer who has already talked to a chef shows up with a case of something specific. A chef who has already talked to a farmer knows what to ask for. An organization that has already compared notes with another one knows where to send the next person who asks.

I would love to see the visit create real business relationships. Farmers meeting buyers. Restaurants finding new suppliers. Organizations working together beyond a single event. And hopefully some money changing hands in the right direction.

## An open call to my network

I know there are farmers, chefs, restaurant owners, food entrepreneurs and people in food distribution all through my network, so here are the questions I am asking.

Who are the Black farmers in our region we should be connecting with? Who is buying locally right now? Who is looking for a new supplier? And who wants to be in the room when the truck arrives?

If you are one of those people, send me a note or answer on the post, and I will do my best to put you in touch with the others. I do not have a date to give you. What I have is a season, a truck with a calendar full of Maryland stops, and a list that is still empty. I would like to see how long it takes to fill.
