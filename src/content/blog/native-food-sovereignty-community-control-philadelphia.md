A seed packet is easy to leave out of a conversation about feeding people. We can count the groceries going out today. The seed belongs to next season, and next season can feel a long way off when someone needs dinner. I understand that urgency. But as we mark [Native American Heritage Month in November](https://www.nativeamericanheritagemonth.gov/), I want us to pay attention to the people doing both kinds of work: meeting food needs and rebuilding their communities' ability to direct what happens next. For a coalition, that raises an uncomfortable and useful question. When we bring resources to a food project, who gets to decide how those resources are used?

## Food sovereignty puts decisions in the community's hands

[First Nations Development Institute describes Native food sovereignty](https://www.firstnations.org/focus-areas/native-food-sovereignty/) through Tribal communities' right to govern their own food systems. That includes authority over food policies and practices. It is a more demanding idea than simply getting enough food into a place. It asks who has the power to make choices about the food system that serves them.

We should take that definition seriously on its own terms. Tribal sovereignty belongs to Native nations; it is not a new name for a Philadelphia nonprofit committee. Learning from this work requires respecting that distinction, including the limits of what outsiders can decide.

For those of us working through [Feed Philly Coalition](https://feedphillycoalition.org/), which brings people across food, civic and community work together, there is still a practical question to bring home. Does a partnership give the people affected real authority, or merely ask for their opinions after the budget is settled? I would like us to ask that before the project has a name, a logo and a launch date.

## Philadelphia's food story begins before the city

The [Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia's account of Native peoples before 1680](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/native-peoples-to-1680/) describes Lenape communities in the lower Delaware Valley before European settlement. Our region's food history did not begin with a colonial market or a modern food bank. Lenape people were feeding communities here before Philadelphia existed.

The [New York Botanical Garden's Lenape Foodways account](https://sweetgum.nybg.org/science/the-hand-lens/explore/narratives-details/?irn=7662) describes cultivated fields and gardens alongside gathered foods. Corn, beans and squash were among the crops; nuts, berries and edible roots were among the foods gathered. I am interested in the organized work that implies: knowing what grows where, tending it and preparing it. Food was already the result of knowledge and effort here.

That is documented food history, rather than a story I need to embellish with a legend. Nor should we put Lenape people entirely in the past. The [Delaware Tribe of Indians' own account](https://delawaretribe.org/about-us/) describes its continuing government, cultural work and community services, as well as the forced moves westward in its history. The people whose food knowledge we acknowledge have living institutions and priorities of their own. A November acknowledgment should leave room for those voices to be heard directly.

## A seed network has work behind the name

The [Native American Food Sovereignty Alliance's Indigenous Seed Keepers Network](https://www.nativefoodalliance.org/programs/iskn) offers a concrete example of what support can look like. Its work includes educational resources, mentoring, help with seed programs, and stipends and small grants for seedkeepers and projects. It also connects people for learning and supports work on seed policy and stewardship.

That is worth slowing down over. A seed collection needs people who know how to care for it. A training needs someone with knowledge and time to teach. A network needs someone answering questions and keeping relationships going after a gathering ends. Those are the kinds of costs I would want a food project budget to recognize.

The lesson I take is to ask what keeps the work possible between the moments an outside supporter can see. If the answer is a grower's time or a coordinator's wages, those belong in the conversation about support. We don't need to turn seed keeping into a Philadelphia project, borrow a crop or claim knowledge that isn't ours. We can read what the network says it needs and support the work it leads.

## Farms, markets and teaching belong in the same conversation

The [Siċaŋġu Food Sovereignty Initiative](https://www.sicangu.co/food) describes its work through seed sovereignty, Keya Wakpala Farms, farmer and rancher education, the Siċaŋġu Harvest Market, and health and cooking classes. Growing, learning, selling and preparing food appear together in the program's own account. That gives us something more useful to study than a general claim that local food is good.

I read that description and want to follow the connections. If a grower needs help finding buyers, another food delivery alone won't answer the question. If someone wants to learn to grow, an occasional box of vegetables won't teach the skill. Those are different needs, and a coalition ought to be able to hold them in the same conversation without pretending one activity does everything.

The photograph accompanying this post comes from another named place: the Institute of American Indian Arts demonstration garden in Santa Fe, New Mexico. [USDA identifies the red amaranth and the garden in the photograph's record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flowers_%2820190911-OPPE-LSC-0575%29.jpg). It is a real teaching garden, not an illustration of a Philadelphia or Lenape site.

[USDA's account of the IAIA demonstration garden](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flowers_%2820190911-OPPE-LSC-0575%29.jpg) describes an outdoor learning space maintained by local Tribal members, students and faculty alongside the college's education staff. It connects agricultural teaching with outreach and research for Native communities. When we talk about food with community control, teaching people the skills to keep growing belongs in that discussion too.

## Support includes the paperwork and the funding

The [Intertribal Agriculture Council's Technical Assistance Network](https://www.indianag.org/technicalassistance) helps Tribes and Tribal producers access USDA programs. Its published work includes assessing eligibility, identifying resources and helping with implementation. Here is another part of a food system that a photograph of a harvest cannot show: the help needed to turn an available program into something a producer can actually use.

Then there is money. [First Nations Development Institute's Native Food Sovereignty Grants](https://www.firstnations.org/projects/gather-food-sovereignty-grants/) support organizations led by Native people in strengthening food systems their communities direct. The stated purpose connects resources with community policies and Tribal food sovereignty. These examples are distinct programs, not a claim that every Native community organizes food work in the same way.

For me, the practical lesson is to fund the part of the work the community identifies. Someone may need equipment. Someone else may need help completing an application, or paid hours to run a program. A supporter should ask before deciding that the contribution has to be a new event or a delivery with a photograph attached.

These are examples we can learn from. I am not describing a partnership between Feed Philly Coalition and these organizations, or claiming that a grant guarantees any particular result. The work deserves attention without attaching a success number we cannot verify.

## Put the question on the next meeting agenda

Yes, we still need to get food to people today. I don't want a conversation about community control to become another reason to delay dinner. The people doing immediate food relief should have the resources to keep doing it.

But urgency can coexist with a plan for who controls the next season. At the next food project meeting, I would ask the people affected to name a decision they want authority over. Then I would ask what money and paid time make that authority workable. Choosing the crops, setting distribution hours or deciding how a budget is spent are possible starting points, depending on the project. Those are proposals for our own work, not a checklist we should impose on a Native organization.

A donor can begin by reading a Native food program's official pages and using its own channels to support it. A coalition organizer can make room in the project budget for community members to do the planning. A purchasing manager can ask growers what would make a buying relationship useful before writing the terms. None of those actions requires pretending we have all the answers.

For this November acknowledgment, read the organizations' own accounts. Choose a practical way to support the priorities they name. Bring that same care to the next Philadelphia food project: ask who gets to decide, and give that answer a place in the budget.
